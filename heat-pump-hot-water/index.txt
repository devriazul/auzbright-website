<p>Suitability depends on the individual property. Landlords, owner-occupiers and property managers should confirm the installation scope and any required approvals before booking.</p>
<h2 id="1-check-how-much-installation-space-is-available">1. Check how much installation space is available</h2>
<p>Townhouses often have smaller outdoor areas than detached homes. The existing hot water system may be positioned in a narrow side passage, courtyard, garage or service area.</p>
<p>Before choosing a heat pump system, check:</p>
<ul>
<li>whether the proposed unit fits the available area</li>
<li>whether there is enough surrounding airflow</li>
<li>whether doors, gates or windows would be obstructed</li>
<li>whether the system can be reached for future servicing</li>
<li>whether plumbing and drainage can be arranged safely</li>
<li>whether installers can move the equipment into position</li>
<li>whether the location affects common property</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not assume that the current gas or electric hot water location will automatically suit a heat pump replacement. A different position may be recommended after the property is assessed.</p>
<h2 id="2-review-airflow-and-ventilation">2. Review airflow and ventilation</h2>
<p>Heat pump hot water systems draw heat from the surrounding air. This means the installation area should not be selected only because it is hidden or convenient.</p>
<p>A tightly enclosed space may not provide the conditions needed for a suitable installation. The installer should review the proposed location, surrounding walls, ventilation, clearances and access.</p>
<p>For a townhouse, this may be particularly important where the system is considered for:</p>
<ul>
<li>a narrow side passage</li>
<li>a covered courtyard</li>
<li>a garage</li>
<li>a small rear yard</li>
<li>an area surrounded by fencing</li>
<li>a space beneath a balcony or overhang</li>
</ul>
<p>The correct location depends on the selected system and the property. Model-specific installation requirements should be confirmed before work begins.</p>
<h2 id="3-think-about-noise-before-selecting-the-location">3. Think about noise before selecting the location</h2>
<p>Heat pump systems use operating components that can produce sound. The actual level depends on the product, installation and operating conditions, so homeowners should not rely on a general statement that every system will be silent.</p>
<p>In a townhouse setting, consider the distance between the proposed system and:</p>
<ul>
<li>bedrooms</li>
<li>living areas</li>
<li>neighbouring properties</li>
<li>shared courtyards</li>
<li>windows</li>
<li>outdoor entertaining areas</li>
<li>boundary fences</li>
</ul>
<p>Ask the provider where the system will be installed and whether its position may affect occupants or neighbours. Product-specific sound information should be reviewed for the proposed model rather than assumed.</p>
<h2 id="4-check-whether-approval-may-be-required">4. Check whether approval may be required</h2>
<p>Some Derrimut townhouses form part of an owners corporation. Others may have shared walls, common driveways, managed external areas or rules affecting visible changes.</p>
<p>Approval may need to be checked where the installation involves:</p>
<ul>
<li>equipment visible from outside the property</li>
<li>pipework fixed to an external wall</li>
<li>work affecting common property</li>
<li>access through shared areas</li>
<li>changes near a common driveway</li>
<li>installation on a wall or surface that may not belong solely to the lot</li>
<li>electrical or plumbing work connected through shared infrastructure</li>
</ul>
<p>Before booking, review the plan of subdivision, owners-corporation rules and property-management requirements. Where necessary, request written approval.</p>
<p>Auzbright can explain the proposed installation scope, but the property owner remains responsible for confirming any owners-corporation, landlord or property-management approvals that apply.</p>
<h2 id="5-confirm-who-is-authorised-to-approve-the-upgrade">5. Confirm who is authorised to approve the upgrade</h2>
<p>Approval can become unclear when the property is rented or managed by another party.</p>
<p>Before requesting installation, confirm whether the decision must involve:</p>
<ul>
<li>the property owner</li>
<li>a landlord</li>
<li>a property manager</li>
<li>an owners corporation</li>
<li>a strata or building manager</li>
<li>a tenant for access arrangements</li>
</ul>
<p>A tenant may help arrange access, but they may not have authority to approve permanent work. Similarly, a property manager may coordinate the quote while final approval remains with the owner.</p>
<p>Clarifying responsibility early can prevent delays after a system has been selected.</p>
<h2 id="6-match-the-system-to-household-hot-water-demand">6. Match the system to household hot water demand</h2>
<p>Townhouse size alone does not determine the right hot water setup. A two-bedroom townhouse occupied by a couple may have different needs from the same type of property occupied by a family.</p>
<p>Before selecting a system, consider:</p>
<ul>
<li>number of occupants</li>
<li>number of bathrooms</li>
<li>peak shower times</li>
<li>laundry and kitchen usage</li>
<li>whether hot water regularly runs out</li>
<li>whether household size may change</li>
<li>whether the property is owner-occupied or rented</li>
</ul>
<p>The system should be selected for realistic daily demand. Avoid choosing only by the lowest price, tank size or promotional offer.</p>
<h2 id="7-review-the-existing-system-and-connection-requirements">7. Review the existing system and connection requirements</h2>
<p>The current hot water system affects the replacement process. Replacing an electric storage unit may involve different work from replacing a gas storage or instantaneous system.</p>
<p>Ask the provider to confirm:</p>
<ul>
<li>what happens to the old system</li>
<li>whether removal is included</li>
<li>whether gas disconnection is required</li>
<li>whether electrical work may be needed</li>
<li>whether the switchboard should be checked</li>
<li>whether plumbing changes are required</li>
<li>where drainage will be directed</li>
<li>whether certificates or compliance documents are included</li>
</ul>
<p>These details can affect the final quote and installation timing.</p>
<h2 id="8-check-eligibility-without-assuming-approval">8. Check eligibility without assuming approval</h2>
<p>Eligible Victorian households may be able to access support for approved heat pump hot water upgrades under current energy-efficiency pathways. However, eligibility is not automatic.</p>
<p>It may depend on:</p>
<ul>
<li>the existing hot water system</li>
<li>the replacement product</li>
<li>current program requirements</li>
<li>property and installation details</li>
<li>provider and installer requirements</li>
<li>required documentation</li>
</ul>
<p>Only suitable and currently eligible products should be considered where a government-supported pathway is involved. Do not rely on claims that every townhouse qualifies or that a particular discount is guaranteed.</p>
<p>Auzbright can help Derrimut homeowners explore available upgrade pathways and understand what information is needed for an eligibility check.</p>

<p>Before accepting it, check whether it includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>product supply</li>
<li>standard installation</li>
<li>old-system removal</li>
<li>plumbing work</li>
<li>electrical work</li>
<li>drainage</li>
<li>required mounting or pipework</li>
<li>certificates and paperwork</li>
<li>eligibility assessment</li>
<li>exclusions</li>
<li>possible site-specific costs</li>
</ul>
<p>The quote should also identify the proposed installation location. This gives the property owner information that may be needed for approval.</p>
